Journal and reviews: how it learns

This is what makes the skill get better the more you use it.

The journal​

Every time Claude scores or reports on a stock, it writes one line in your journal: the date, the price, the verdict, the score and a one-line reason.

2026-10-23  AAPL     Positive signals, earnings soon  @ 346.61  score +35  Strong margins, earnings in 10 days

See it any time:

Show my stock journal
What did we say about AAPL before?

Reviews​

Until then, the dashboard's Journal shows how a young idea is doing so far, for example "leaning right" with "day 5 of 30" under it. It's a hint, not a result: the idea is only judged once it's 30 days old.

After a call is 30 days old, it can be checked. Ask:

Review how our past calls did

For each old call, Claude compares:

  • how much the stock moved since the call, with
  • how much the market moved over the same time (the S&P 500 for US stocks, the Nifty 50 for Indian stocks).
CallCounted as right when…
When you track an idea in the dashboard, you also give your own view: beat, match or lag the index. Your view is judged by the same rules, so the Journal shows you vs the tool, including how you did when you disagreed with it. From Claude, add `--view positivemixed
Label or viewCounted as right when…
Positive signalsthe stock beat the market
Negative signalsthe stock trailed the market
Mixed signals (and "earnings soon")the stock stayed within 3% of the market

Calls logged before October 2026 use the old labels BUY-lean, SELL-lean and HOLD. They mean the same and are reviewed the same way.

You get a hit rate, for example "Hit rate: 7/10 (70%)".

The tool's record​

The Journal's Tool's record view shows how the tool's own labels have done, not just the ideas you chose to track. Every time a symbol is scanned, its label (Positive, Mixed or Negative signals) is saved for that day. Thirty days later it is checked the same way as a tracked idea: Positive signals are right if the stock beat its index, Negative signals if it lagged it, and Mixed signals if it stayed within 3% of it.

  • One call per symbol per 30 days. A stock scanned every day would otherwise count the same call again and again, which makes a record look surer than it is. The first label counts, then the next one at least 30 days later.
  • Right out of checked, by label, with the average return against the index for each label. A rate shows from 5 calls; with fewer, luck dominates.
  • The latest 20 checked calls, with the price then and 30 days later, the index over the same days, and the result.

Published record. Above your own, the view shows the same check on a fixed list of about 150 large US and Indian stocks. Chatoor scores that list every market day after the US close and saves each label with that day's closing price; each new version of the app carries the results so far, so everyone on that version sees the same numbers. Until its first labels are 30 days old it says how many are waiting and when the first check is due.

Your own record uses only the symbols scanned in your copy, from daily closing prices (not adjusted for dividends, for the stock and the index alike). Until the first labels are 30 days old it says when the first check is due. Past results don't say what the next call will do.

The entry and exit rule​

When you track an idea, its entry and exit levels are saved with it. The Journal's Rule column then replays the daily highs and lows since the call and shows what happened:

Rule columnMeaning
waiting to enterthe price hasn't come back to the entry zone yet
in the tradeit entered, and has reached neither the stop nor the first target
target firstit reached the first target before the stop
stop firstit reached the stop first. A day that touched both counts as the stop, because daily prices can't show which came first
never entered / neither in 90 daysthe rule gets 90 days; after that, the trade is over without a result

This doesn't wait 30 days: a trade can finish sooner. The line above the table adds up the finished trades, for example "the target came first in 3 of 5 finished trades (60%)". That's how you see whether the rule is any good. Ideas tracked before version 0.4.0 have no levels.

The rule on past prices​

The rule only reads prices, so it can also be tested on history. The Journal's Tool's record shows it replayed on about 150 large US and Indian stocks over the last five years, on a test date every 63 trading days (about a quarter): on each date the numbers the rule uses are rebuilt from the year of prices before it, the rule gives its levels or says it would wait, and the following 90 days are played out as above. Results are measured against the amount at risk (the entry price minus the stop): reaching a target twice that far away makes twice the amount at risk, and the stop loses all of it.

In the version shipped, the first target came before the stop in about half the finished trades, roughly break-even on average before costs. Since version 0.17 the rule only plans a pullback in an uptrend: on past prices, pullbacks without one lost money, and that held on the later years the change was checked against. The Journal counts ideas tracked under the earlier version of the rule apart. The rule did better when it waited for a close above the 50-day average than on pullbacks. The analysis panel's Entry and exit section shows the line for the setup it's describing.

The test is kinder than real life: the stocks are today's large companies (which did well enough to become large), prices that jumped past a level count at the level, there are no costs, and past analyst targets aren't known, so one resistance level the live rule can use is left out.

What the panel said, and what followed​

Tracking an idea also saves what the analysis panel said about it: the four questions (the business, the price, the trend, what would change it), the four pictures of the business, and whether the stock was in the deep-fall setup. Once ideas are reviewed, the Journal's What the panel said, and what followed groups them by each answer: how many beat their index, and the middle result against it.

For example, "The price: Low, beat the index 4 of 6 (67%)". It's how you find out whether an answer has meant anything for the stocks you look at. A rate shows from 5 ideas; below that you see only the count, because a few cases are mostly luck. It stays on your computer and is not part of the Research export. Ideas tracked before version 0.8.0 didn't save it.

Lessons​

Claude then looks for patterns in the misses and writes them down as lessons. For example:

  • 2026-12-01: Momentum-driven positive calls on small caps failed 3 of 4 times. Be more careful with small caps. (evidence: journal ids …)

It reads these lessons before every analysis, so it doesn't repeat the same mistake.

You add lessons too. Once an idea has been checked, press Look back next to it in the dashboard's Journal. It shows what you expected and your reason, next to what happened, and asks which it was: right for your reasons, right by luck, reasons that didn't hold, or sound reasoning with bad luck. A good result can come from bad reasons, so this is how you learn from single ideas without fooling yourself. Your answer is added to your lessons, for example:

  • 2026-12-01 KO (2026-10-17-KO-3): Right, but mostly luck. My reason was: steady dividend. Next time: check the dividend date first

Rewind: practise on the past​

Waiting 30 days to find out whether you were right is slow. The Rewind tab gives you a result in seconds:

  1. Press Start a round. You see a real stock as it stood on a random day one to four years ago: a year of its price next to its index, and the numbers from that day (6-month return against the index, distance from the 200-day average, RSI, distance from the 52-week high). Its name and the date are hidden, so you can't answer from memory. Pick US stocks, Indian stocks or both.
  2. Say whether it did better than, about the same as, or worse than its index over the next 30 days, and how sure you are.
  3. Lock in my call reveals the stock, the date, the next 30 days on the chart, whether you were right, and what the tool's price-only read would have said.

Your record against the tool's and How sure vs. how right build up below. Rounds use price data only, because Yahoo doesn't keep company figures from back then. They're saved in ~/.stock-analyst/rewind.json and included in the Research export.

How sure vs. how right​

When you track an idea you say how sure you are (50% to 100%). Once 5 of those ideas have been checked, the Journal shows How sure vs. how right: how sure you were on average against how often you were right, and a row for each confidence level (for example "90% sure: right 1 of 2"). If you're right much less often than you said, you're overconfident, which is the most common beginner pattern. Few ideas per row means a lot of luck, so read it as a trend, not a grade.

Taking part in a study​

Studies help work out how people learn to invest. Taking part takes about 10 minutes a week for six weeks, it's anonymous, and everyone who takes part gets a copy of the findings. If a researcher invites you to a study, they send you a study code like E-7K3Q-1. Joining only appears once the researcher has opened the study in your version of the tool; until then the panel offers just the Research export. At the bottom of the dashboard's Journal, enter it and press Join the study (if it's mistyped, the panel says so; codes are a letter, four characters and a number). Then:

  • Two three-week blocks. The tool turns Guess first on for one block and off for the other (your code decides which comes first). While the study runs, you can't change it yourself.
  • Three short check-ins: before you start, halfway and at the end. Five quick questions (guessing is fine) and how confident you feel; the later ones also ask what you think of the tool (only about features you've used by then), with optional comments.
  • "How sure are you?" Everyone, in or out of a study, picks 50% to 100% when tracking an idea.
  • Daily counts of what you used (scans, analyses opened, ideas tracked, look-backs, Learn entries, practice trades): numbers only, no symbols or times.

Nothing leaves your computer until you choose how. If the researcher set up a study database, the Journal asks once: Send automatically each week (the app sends your anonymous export once a week and straight after each check-in, while it's open) or I'll send it myself (press Send now, and see everything first). You can pause or turn on automatic sending at any time; the study panel shows when it was last sent, when it goes next, and if a send didn't go through. It's the same anonymous export either way: Research export shows exactly what it holds. Without a study database you send the Research export file yourself, and Leave the study deletes what you sent from the database too. Leave the study deletes your study code, check-ins and counts; your journal and practice account stay.

Researchers make balanced codes with python3 scripts/research.py codes --experienced 25 --novice 25.

Research export​

At the bottom of the dashboard's Journal there's Research export. It makes an anonymous file of your learning data for you to send to the researcher yourself; nothing is uploaded.

  • In it: a random participant code (like P-7K3Q9X, the same every time on this computer), each idea's date, symbol, the tool's label and score, your view and how sure you were, whether you made it with Guess first on, the results, and your Look back choice; your Start progress; and a summary of your practice account (number of trades and its return). If you joined a study: your study code, group and day, your check-in answers (including any comments you wrote there) and the daily counts.
  • Never in it: your reasons and notes (only whether you wrote one), real holdings, day trades, limits, keys, your symbol list, your name or anything that identifies you.

You see everything in the file before it's saved. Only send it if you've agreed to take part in a study.

Tuning the score​

When there's enough evidence (at least 5 reviewed calls), Claude may suggest changing how much each factor counts. For example: "Momentum misled us in 4 of 6 misses. Lower its weight from 20 to 15?" It only changes this if you say yes, and it logs every change.

Your profile​

Claude also keeps a short profile of how you invest: your markets, how long you hold, your style, how long you like reports. It fills this in naturally as you chat. You never have to fill in a form. Want to set it straight away? Just say:

I'm a long-term investor in Indian stocks, I like short reports

:::tip Make it a habit Ask for a review once a month. The more calls are reviewed, the more useful the lessons become. :::
